# NIAS HISTORY TIMELINE

## I. The Ancient Prehistory (c. 55,000 Years Ago – c. 1450 AD)

### c. 55,000 Years Ago: Early Stepping Stones

Modern humans begin migrating from Africa to Australia and Melanesia. Nias, situated within the long chain of the Barrier Islands off the Sumatra coast, serves as a stepping stone during this early pre-Neolithic maritime migration.[1]

### c. 12,000 Years Ago: First Continuous Human Occupation

Archaeological excavations in Tögi Ndrawa cave (located in North Nias, near Gunungsitoli) reveal continuous human occupation during the Paleolithic/Neolithic boundary. These early cave-dwelling hunter-gatherers belong to the Hoabinhian culture.[2]

Mythological Connection: In Nias oral literature, these ancient cave dwellers are preserved as Laturadanö and Ono Mbela (tree-dwelling spirits), representing the indigenous populations who inhabited the island before the main Austronesian migrations.[3]

### c. 4,500 – 3,000 Years Ago: The Austronesian Expansion

Agriculturalists originating from Formosa (now Taiwan) migrate southward via the Philippines into Island Southeast Asia.[4] Modern DNA studies of more than 400 contemporary Niasans reveal that they are almost 100% genetically homogeneous, carrying distinct Austronesian paternal markers (O-M119\* and O-M110).[5]

The Replacement Mystery: DNA evidence shows that the original 12,000-year-old Hoabinhian cave dwellers left no genetic trace in modern Niasans. This points to a complete population replacement, likely caused by diseases brought by the agriculturalists to which the cave dwellers had no immunity.[6]

### c. 1200 – 1450 AD: The Arrival of "New Immigrants"

A highly organized, agriculturalist group enters Nias, bringing advanced metallurgy, iron tools, golden jewelry, and sophisticated wooden carving traditions.[7]

The Birth of Earthly Settlements: According to Nias mythology, the first ancestors descend from the primeval village of Tetehöli Ana'a, which is the first settlement in Börönadu, Gomo, and became the place of origin of the modern Nias people.[8]

The forefather Tuada Hia (posthumously named Lawa Lani or Lowalangi) establishes Börönadu in the mountainous Gomo area (Central Nias), which is celebrated as the cradle of Nias civilization.[9]

From this legendary village, Hia's descendants migrated to different directions in Nias: Gözö went to Hilimaziaya (North), Daeli to Ono Waembo Idanoi (East), and Hulu to the banks of the Oyo River (West).[10]

## II. Early Written Records (c. 1st Century AD – 14th Century AD)

### c. 1st Century AD onwards: The "Barrier Islands" of Sumatra

Early Roman, Greek, and Indian maritime geographical accounts make sporadic mentions of the long chain of islands situated off the western coast of Sumatra, acknowledging their location along early trading routes.[11]

### 851 AD: The First Explicit Written Witness

The Persian/Arabic merchant Sulayman records the earliest explicit description of Nias under the name "Niyan." He notes that the island is rich in gold, relies heavily on coconuts for food, produces palm wine, and has a defensive society where headhunting is a strict prerequisite for men to marry.[12]

### 950 AD: Nias Head Hunters

The traveler Bozorg van Ramhormoz writes about Nias, documenting that the islanders boast of captured heads and value brass objects as highly as gold for bartering.[13]

### 1154 AD: Nias Highly Populated

The famous Arab geographer Al-Edrisi records that Nias ("Niyan") is highly populated, features a major urban settlement, and is inhabited by extremely brave warriors who honor and glorify headhunters as war heroes.[14]

### 1260 AD: Light-Skinned People of Nias

The scholar Al-Kazwini describes the inhabitants of Nias as beautiful, light-skinned people living in the high mountain interiors, who hide from foreign traders and use simple loincloths or bark skirts.[15]

### 1310 AD: Nias and Trade Connection

Persian physician and historian Rashid ad-Din mentions Nias in his historical records, noting its commercial ties with the camphor-exporting port of Barus in Sumatra.[16]

## III. Megalithic Tradition & Regional Migrations (c. 1500 – c. 1760s)

### c. 1500 AD: The Evolution of Megaliths & Wooden "Adu"

The Transition of Carving: According to Gomo oral history, the first two ancestral figures in Börönadu Gomo were made of iron. When they were lost, the people began to carve ancestral images (adu zatua) from wood.[17]

A Young Stone Tradition: While Nias is famous for its grand megalithic stone monuments, archaeological excavations show that this megalithic tradition is young. Most of the massive stone monuments (erected during grand merit-feasts, or owasa, to honor chiefs) are only 200 to 500 years old.[18]

Southward Migration: Overpopulation and inter-clan disputes prompt massive migrations from the Gomo cradle (Central Nias) southward into the South Nias hills, where magnificent fortified villages are built.[19]

### 1660s: First Contact with European Traders (The VOC)

The Dutch East India Company (VOC) begins trading with the coastal chiefs of Nias to barter for forest products, copra, and pigs.[20]

### 1669: The VOC Monopoly Contracts

The VOC signs formal commercial treaties with several powerful chiefs (salaŵa in North Nias as well as in Hinako Island and si'ulu in South Nias) to secure trade monopolies.[21]

### 1693: The Rise of the Slaving Ports

Finding regular trade unprofitable, the VOC shifts to the slave trade, exchanging gold for captives with coastal chiefs to supply agricultural labor for Sumatran plantations. The VOC establishes a fortified trading storehouse and office in Gunungsitoli.[22]

### c. 1760s: Migration to the Batu Islands

Groups from South Nias migrate to the southern Batu Islands, carrying Nias culture and language with them, eventually assimilating the indigenous Buginese (Maru) traders already present there.[23]

## IV. The Dark Age of Slavery & Early European Struggles (1790 – 1864)

### 1790 – 1830: The Peak of the Slave Trade

West Sumatran, Acehnese, Chinese, and European slave traders intensify raids on Nias. Approximately 800 to 1,500 Niasans (0.4% of the island's population) are sold into slavery annually. This creates deep tribal warfare, headhunting raids (emali), and the relocation of villages to high, fortified hilltops.[24]

### 1819 – 1820: British Anti-Slavery Intervention

British administrators John Prince and William Jack visit Nias to enforce the British ban on the selling of slaves. While the British prohibit the slave trade, they temporarily permit local chiefs to keep slaves as long as they are not sold.[25]

### 1824: The Treaty of London

The British cede Sumatra and Nias to the Dutch. The Dutch establish their first official administrator (posthouder) in Gunungsitoli, but abandon it by 1826, leaving Nias open to Acehnese slave raiders.[26]

### 1831 – 1832: The First Catholic Mission

French Catholic missionaries Jean-Pierre Vallon and Jean-Laurent Bérard, accompanied by a Niasan catechist couple (Francisco and Sophie), land on Nias from Penang. They settle in Lasara (near Gunungsitoli) but die of malaria within months, ending early Catholic efforts.[27]

### 1834: The Failed First Protestan Mission

Protestant missionaries Samuel Munson and Henry Lyman from Boston explore the eastern coast of Nias but are denied permission by the Dutch to settle. They sail to Sumatra, enter the Bataklands, and are killed.[28]

### 1847: The Burning of Lagundri

After Dutch mapping official Donleben is attacked, Dutch warships bombard and burn the southern village of Lagundri, erecting a temporary military fortress on the ruins.[29]

### 1855: The Orahili War (First Dutch Defeat)

Dutch troops under Captain De Voss attack Orahili village to enforce colonial mapping. The brave warriors of Orahili completely defeat the Dutch army, marking the first successful Niasan military resistance against European powers.[30]

### 1863: The Great Dutch Purge

A massive Dutch expeditionary force of 600 soldiers led by Major Fritzen systematically invades South Nias. The villages of Mbotohösi, Orahili, Lölöwa'u, and Lagundri are burned. To control the region, the Dutch establish a restricted Government Protected Area (rapatgebied) around Gunungsitoli, leaving the rest of the island under sovereign chiefs.[31]

## V. The Arrival of the Protestant Missionaries & Direct Colonial Rule (1865 – 1914)

### September 27, 1865: The Birth of the Nias Church

German missionary Ludwig Ernst Denninger of the Rhenish Missionary Society (RM) arrives in Gunungsitoli. This date is celebrated today as the birthday of Nias's Protestant Church.[32]

### Easter Sunday, 1874: The First Fruits

After nine years of difficult work, the first 25 Niasan converts are baptized in Gunungsitoli by Denninger and Friedrich Kramer, after formally surrendering their ancestral adu zatua statues.[33]

### 1880: The Dahana Conference

Heinrich Sundermann chairs the first official Nias Missionary Conference in Dahana. The first indigenous Niasan teacher-preacher (guru) and elder (satua) are appointed, laying the foundations for local church leadership.[34]

### 1889: The Batu Islands Mission Begins

The Dutch Lutheran Missionary Society (DLM) begins pioneering work on the Batu Islands under German missionary C.W. Frickenschmidt.[35]

### 1892: Sirombu Station Founded

Missionary August Lett founds the first mission station on the West Coast of Nias (originally Fadoro, later renamed Sirombu). Lett successfully adapts to Niasan customs by sharing afo (betel nut) and eating with his hands.[36]

### January 29, 1908: The Fall of South Nias

The Dutch Resident of Tapanuli is attacked in the Upper Susua Valley. The Dutch react with overwhelming military force, marching into the interior and completely subjugating the sovereign chiefs of Nias.[37]

### 1908: The Felling of the Sacred Fösi-Tree

Missionary Johannes Bieger fells the ancient, sacred fösi tree of Bawalia (the mythological tree of ancestral origin). The destruction of this religious symbol, coinciding with Dutch military victory, shatters the spiritual stronghold of the primal religion.[38]

### 1908: Introduction of Forced Labor (Rodi)

The Dutch enforce the rodi system. Niasans are forced to build roads, plant coconuts, relocate their villages from fortified hills to lowland roads, and move pigsties from underneath traditional houses. This breaks the traditional autarchic village system.[39]

### 1912: The Bible in Nias Language

Heinrich Sundermann completes the first complete translation of the Bible (Soera Ni'amoni'ö) into the Nias language.[40]

### 1914: The Training of Niasan Ministers

Eduard Fries initiates the first theological courses at the Ombölata seminary to train Niasan ministers (fandrita Niha).[41]

## VI. The Great Awakening, Autonomy & War (1915 – 1945)

### 1915: Compilation of Christian Adat

The Dutch colonial administration, in cooperation with the mission, publishes a special legal codex of Christian adat laws (Adat-Recht) for Nias, regulating wedding dowries (böwö) and social interactions.[42]

### Late 1915 – 1930: The Great Awakening (Fangesa Dödö Sebua)

A massive religious revival sweeps across Nias in several "waves." Initiated by Niasans themselves (beginning with assistant-teacher Filemo in Helefanikha), the movement is characterized by deep mass confession of sins, public repentance, and the ultimate destruction of the remaining adu zatua ancestral figures. The number of baptized Christians quadruples from 17,700 (1914) to over 84,000 (1930).[43]

### 1935: The Conversion of Börö Nadu Gomo

The last major public stronghold of the primal religion, the sacred sanctuary of Tuada Hia in Börö Nadu, Gomo, formally embraces Christianity, building a church on the site.[44]

### November 1936: The Founding of the BNKP

The Nias church holds its first independent synod in Gunungsitoli, establishing the Banoea Niha Keriso Protestant ba danö Nias (B.N.K.P.-Nias). This marks the transition to ecclesiastical self-governance.[45]

### May 1940: German Internment

With the outbreak of World War II in Europe, the Dutch colonial government interns all German Protestant missionaries on Nias, transferring leadership of the church completely to Niasan office-bearers.[46]

### April 17, 1942: Japanese Invasion

The Imperial Japanese Army invades Nias (invading the Batu Islands on April 29). Japanese occupation brings extreme suffering, famine, and forced labor. The administrative division of Nias is restructured under Japanese constitutional rules.[47]

## VII. Modern Era: Independence & Consolidation (1945 – Present)

### August 15, 1945: The Defeat of Japan

Japan surrenders, ending World War II.

### October 6, 1945: Indonesian Independence Reaches Nias

News of the Indonesian Proclamation of Independence (August 17) reaches Nias. Educated Niasans and church leaders eagerly declare their loyalty to the new Republic of Indonesia, blockading cities to prevent the return of Dutch administrators.[48]

### 1949: Reverend Ros Telaumbanua Appointed Regent

Under the newly consolidated Republic of Indonesia, Nias and the Batu Islands are officially united as the Nias Regency. Protestant Reverend Ros Telaumbanua is appointed the first Bupati (Regent) of Nias.[49]

### 1950s: The Catholic Mission Return

After more than a century, the Roman Catholic mission returns to Nias, establishing schools, clinics, and a major catechist training center in Hilisimaetanö under Father Van der Weijden.[50]

### September 27, 1965: The Centennial Jubilee

The BNKP celebrates 100 years of the Gospel on Nias in Gunungsitoli, receiving massive ecumenical foreign aid, including schools, dormitories, a shipping vessel (Agape), and new church buildings.[51]

### 1991: Founding of Nias Heritage Foundation & Museum

Johannes Hämmerle establishes the Nias Heritage Foundation (Yayasan Pusaka Nias), which operates the Nias Heritage Museum, the first institution dedicated to preserving Nias cultural heritage.

### 2013: Reclaiming the Nias Narrative

Johannes Hämmerle publishes the essay "150 Years of Ethnological Interpretation and Misinterpretation on the Example of Nias, Indonesia."

This essay sought to rectify prevailing misinterpretations that had become dominant narratives about Nias in academia and public consciousness. For a decade, Hämmerle's critique went largely unheeded. Through the Nias Heritage initiative, we strive to rekindle that torch and carry forward his legacy of cultural truth.

### Present & Beyond: A Living, Unfolding History

The history of Nias continues to unfold.
